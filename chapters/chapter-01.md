# Chapter One: The Aftermath

JANUARY 15, 2024, AETHCOM MEDICAL BAY, UNDISCLOSED LOCATION, CONTINENTAL UNITED STATES

Forty-seven ceiling tiles.

I counted them three times while the medic stitched my shoulder back together. Industrial grey, each one identical to the last. The kind of detail that exists purely to give patients something to focus on besides the needle pulling through their flesh, the hum of fluorescent lights, the recycled air that tastes like antiseptic and old fear.

Standard AETHCOM medical bay. Buried deep underground. No windows, no natural light, nothing to remind you that a world still exists above. My shattered leg was already braced, the bone knitting slowly under Aether-assisted regeneration. My ribs were wrapped tight enough to make breathing an exercise in discipline. The gash on my lip had stopped bleeding an hour ago.

I felt nothing.

Not the pain. Not the needle. Not the burn of antiseptic in open wounds. Just a hollow silence where emotions should have been—the same silence that had always lived there, if I was being honest with myself. Other people seemed to experience distress in situations like this. Fear. Relief at survival. Something.

But then again, I had never been other people.

The medic's hands trembled. I noticed. I always noticed. The way he avoided my eyes. The way he flinched each time I shifted on the table. The way his breath stuttered whenever static crackled through the intercom.

He wasn't afraid of what was happening outside. He was afraid of me.

Fascinating, in its own way. The psychological literature would suggest that individuals who have just survived near-death experiences typically inspire sympathy, not terror. Yet here was a trained medical professional, hands shaking so badly he could barely thread the suture, treating me like I might bite.

To be fair, I had just killed an angel. Perhaps word had already spread.

"All AETHCOM personnel, condition SERAPH. Repeat, condition SERAPH. This is not a drill."

The broadcast came through while they were wrapping my torso. The medic's hands went still. I had been in AETHCOM for eighteen months—long enough to learn the alert tiers. CHERUB was elevated. THRONE was active engagement. SERAPH was—

"What's SERAPH?"

The medic didn't answer. His face had draind of all color.

Interesting. In my experience, medical personnel maintained composure under pressure as a professional requirement. The fact that a single code word could reduce this one to catatonic silence suggested the situation had escalated beyond standard parameters.

The door swung open.

Lieutenant Vasquez stepped through—compact, sharp-eyed, the kind of officer who survived by never being caught off guard. I had observed him in briefings before. Efficient. Competent. The type who followed protocols because protocols existed for reasons.

He looked caught off guard now.

"Blackwell. You're being moved."

"Yes, sir."

Vasquez crossed the room in four strides, grabbed my tactical jacket from the chair, and threw it at my chest. The impact sent fire lancing through my broken ribs. I caught it anyway. I didn't flinch.

Pain is information. Nothing more.

"What's SERAPH?" I asked again.

Vasquez met my eyes. Something flickered there—something I had never seen from him before. Not anger. Not disappointment.

Fear.

"Global divine response." His voice was quiet. Controlled. The voice of a man forcing himself to deliver information he didn't want to believe. "Every angel on the planet just stopped what they were doing. All of them. At the same time."

I pulled on my jacket. The motion hurt. I let it.

"Stopped doing what?"

Vasquez was already at the door. "Everything."



The corridor was chaos.

Personnel ran in both directions. Radios crackled with overlapping transmissions. Somewhere behind us, shouting—not orders, just noise. The sound of people who had run out of protocols.

I catalogued the fragments as I limped after Vasquez:

*Pacific Fleet just went dark, no response on any channel.*

*Ramstein reports grounded, repeat, all aircraft grounded.*

*They're not moving, they're just standing there, like they're waiting—*

Vasquez walked fast. My broken leg screamed with every step. I kept pace anyway.

This was, objectively speaking, a disaster. The kind of systemic failure that organizational theorists write papers about. Multiple simultaneous points of collapse across global military infrastructure, triggered by—what, exactly? Divine intervention? Coordinated angelic response?

If so, the timing was instructive. I had killed an angel approximately four hours ago. Now every angel on the planet had stopped moving.

Correlation is not causation. But in my experience, when the universe presents you with a coincidence this convenient, you should probably assume it's not a coincidence at all.

We passed a break room. Inside, a cluster of analysts stood frozen around a wall-mounted screen. Civilian broadcast, still functioning somehow. The anchor's voice was steady, professional—trained to project calm during catastrophe.

"—unprecedented phenomenon occurring across every continent. Reports indicate that entities previously engaged in enforcement actions have ceased all movement. Governments worldwide are attempting to establish—"

Static swallowed the feed.

Then the screen changed.

A face appeared. Not human. Too perfect, too symmetrical, beautiful in a way that made the eyes ache to look at it. Light burned behind its features—light that shouldn't have been able to transmit through a digital signal.

It spoke a single word.

The sound wasn't language. It was *pressure*. A weight that pushed through the speakers and settled behind my eyes like fingers pressing into my skull. The analysts dropped where they stood. Vasquez stumbled, caught himself on the doorframe. Somewhere down the corridor, someone began to scream.

I felt it.

And I absorbed it.

The pressure hit me like a wave and vanished. Swallowed by the void inside my chest—the same void that had always been there, the emptiness I had learned to weaponize. When my vision cleared, I was the only one still standing.

The screen blazed with golden fire. Then it went dark.

Vasquez was on his knees, blood streaming from his nose. "What—" His voice cracked. "What the fuck was that?"

I reached down and pulled him up. He stared at me like he was seeing a stranger wearing a familiar face.

"You didn't fall," Vasquez whispered.

"No."

"Everyone falls. That was... that was *Him*. That was..."

"I know what it was."

I started walking. After a moment, Vasquez followed, his steps unsteady.

Behind us, the analysts were beginning to stir. Some were crying. Some were praying. Standard trauma responses, according to the literature. I filed the observation away and kept moving.



AETHCOM COMMAND CENTER, TWENTY MINUTES LATER

The command center was built to survive anything.

Underground. Reinforced. Hardened against nuclear strike and divine intervention alike. The kind of room where the last orders would be given when everything else had failed.

It wouldn't survive this. Nothing would.

The main screen displayed a global map. Red markers bloomed across every continent—each one a disabled installation, a grounded fleet, a silenced command post. The map was hemorrhaging crimson.

I performed a quick mental calculation. Based on the density of markers, approximately seventy-eight percent of known military assets had gone dark within the past hour. The remaining twenty-two percent were concentrated in four geographic clusters: North America, Russia, China, and Japan.

The four nations with Aether weapons programs.

Interesting.

General Morrison stood at the center of the room, phone pressed to his ear. His face might have been carved from granite. He wasn't speaking—just listening.

Then he set the phone down. The room went quiet.

"Joint Chiefs just confirmed." His voice was hollow. Scraped clean of emotion. "Naval Command. Strategic Air. CENTCOM. EUCOM. PACOM." He looked at the bleeding map. "They're not responding to us anymore."

Silence stretched like a held breath.

"What about—" someone started.

"No one's responding." Morrison's voice cut through the room. "No one. Every asset we had outside this facility is either dark or disabled. Everything we built." He paused. "*Gone.*"

I stepped forward. Heads turned. I saw it in their faces—that same look. Not anger. Not blame.

Fear.

They were afraid of me. Every single one of them. The woman who had killed an angel and brought Heaven's wrath down on their heads.

Good. Fear was useful. Fear meant they understood the stakes.

"Not everything," I said.

Morrison's eyes found mine. The general who had signed off on my recruitment. Who had watched my test results with clinical interest rather than horror. Who had told me, eighteen months ago, that I was exactly the kind of weapon America needed.

"Blackwell." His voice was flat. "You killed an angel tonight."

"Yes, sir."

"And this—" He gestured at the bleeding map, the dead radios, the controlled panic filling the room. "This is because of you."

I didn't look away. Didn't apologize. Didn't explain.

What would be the point? The facts were self-evident. I had driven a knife through an angel's heart, and God had responded by dismantling humanity's capacity to resist. Cause and effect. Action and consequence.

The only question that mattered now was what we did next.

"Yes, sir," I said again.

Morrison stared at me for a long moment. Then, impossibly, he laughed.

It was a broken sound—the laugh of a man watching the world burn and finding something darkly funny in the flames. The kind of laugh that meant all his carefully constructed expectations about reality had just been incinerated, and he was still trying to figure out what to build from the ashes.

"Well," he said. "At least we know they can die."



THE QUIET HOURS BEFORE DAWN

I sat alone in a corner of the command center and listened to the world come apart.

Radio chatter. Emergency broadcasts. Fragments of a dying order bleeding through speakers no one had thought to mute.

*"Confirmed, the Vatican is saying He appeared, physically manifested—"*

*"China's still fighting, they have something, Aether-based weapons—"*

*"Casualties in the millions, no response from international relief—"*

*"Japan's holding, don't know how, some kind of containment field—"*

*"Russia's gone dark but we're picking up weapons signatures, someone's still shooting—"*

I absorbed it all. Catalogued it. Filed it away in the cold, ordered spaces of my mind.

The world was ending. Governments collapsing. Billions of people looking up at a sky that had just proven their prayers had a listener—and that listener was furious.

Because of me.

Because I had driven a knife through an angel's heart and shown the universe that gods could bleed.

A rational person might have felt guilt at this point. Horror, perhaps. The weight of responsibility for triggering what appeared to be the end of human civilization as we knew it.

I felt something else entirely.

*Satisfaction.*

Not because I enjoyed suffering—that would be pathological. But because my hypothesis had been correct. Angels could die. Divine intervention followed predictable patterns. God's response, while devastating, was *measurable*. Which meant it was *manageable*.

The Stanford Prison Experiment demonstrated that human behavior was dependent on environment. The same principle applied here. God had created an environment of overwhelming force. Humanity had adapted to that environment by surrendering.

But environments could be changed. Rules could be exploited. Systems could be broken.

I just had to figure out how.

A technician approached my station. Young. Pale. He set a cup of coffee beside me that I hadn't asked for, and his hands shook badly enough to slosh liquid over the rim.

"Ma'am." His voice was barely a whisper. "What... what happens now?"

I looked at the map. Red everywhere. Cities dark. Nations silent.

But not everywhere. Four points of light remained. Four nations still transmitting. Four nations with Aether technology and the will to use it. Four nations that hadn't learned how to kneel.

I picked up the coffee. Took a sip. It was bitter and lukewarm—exactly what I expected from a facility running on emergency power and terror.

"Now," I said, "we find out what God does when He's angry."

The technician took a few step back in disbelief.

I smiled.

It wasn't a kind smile. It wasn't meant to be.

After all, this was just the beginning. The opening move in a game that would determine the fate of every human being on the planet. And I had just painted a target on my soul that would never wash off.

But that was fine.

I had never been afraid of consequences. Only of irrelevance.

And no one—not God, not His angels, not the terrified masses praying for salvation—could ever call me irrelevant again.
