# Chapter One: The Aftermath

The fluorescent lights hummed. Lilith counted ceiling tiles while a medic stitched her shoulder back together. Forty-seven tiles. Industrial grey. Standard AETHCOM medical bay—underground, windowless, the air tasting of recirculation and antiseptic. Her shattered leg was already braced, the bone knitting slowly under Aether-assisted regeneration. Her ribs were wrapped tight. The gash on her lip had stopped bleeding an hour ago.

She felt nothing. Not the pain. Not the needle pulling through flesh. Not the burn of antiseptic in open wounds. Just a hollow silence where emotions should have been.

The medic's hands trembled. She noticed. She always noticed. The way he avoided her eyes. The way he flinched each time she shifted on the table. The way his breath stuttered whenever static crackled through the intercom. He wasn't afraid of what was happening outside. He was afraid of her.

The first broadcast came through while they were wrapping her torso. "All AETHCOM personnel, condition SERAPH. Repeat, condition SERAPH. This is not a drill."

The medic's hands went still. Lilith had been in AETHCOM for eighteen months—long enough to learn the alert tiers. CHERUB was elevated. THRONE was active engagement. SERAPH was—

"What's SERAPH?"

The medic didn't answer. His face had gone the color of old concrete.

The door swung open. Lieutenant Vasquez stepped through—compact, sharp-eyed, the kind of officer who survived by never being caught off guard. She looked caught off guard now.

"Blackwell. You're being moved."

"I'm not finished." Three stitches to go.

"Yes, you are."

Vasquez crossed the room in four strides, grabbed Lilith's tactical jacket from the chair, and threw it at her chest. The impact sent fire lancing through her broken ribs. She caught it anyway. She didn't flinch.

"What's SERAPH?" Lilith asked again.

Vasquez met her eyes. Something flickered there—something Lilith had never seen from her before. Not anger. Not disappointment. Fear.

"Global divine response," Vasquez said quietly. "Every angel on the planet just stopped what they were doing. All of them. At the same time."

Lilith pulled on her jacket. The motion hurt. She let it. "Stopped doing what?"

Vasquez was already at the door. "Everything."

The corridor was chaos. Personnel ran in both directions. Radios crackled with overlapping transmissions. Somewhere behind them, shouting—not orders, just noise. The sound of people who had run out of protocols. Fragments reached her as she limped after Vasquez. Pacific Fleet just went dark, no response on any channel. Ramstein reports grounded, repeat, all aircraft grounded. They're not moving, they're just standing there, like they're waiting.

Vasquez walked fast. Lilith's broken leg screamed with every step. She kept pace anyway.

They passed a break room. Inside, a cluster of analysts stood frozen around a wall-mounted screen. Civilian broadcast—still functioning somehow. The anchor's voice was steady, professional, trained to project calm during catastrophe. Unprecedented phenomenon occurring across every continent. Reports indicate that entities previously engaged in enforcement actions have ceased all movement. Governments worldwide are attempting to establish—

Static swallowed the feed. Then the screen changed.

A face appeared. Not human. Too perfect, too symmetrical—beautiful in a way that made the eyes ache to look at it. Light burned behind its features, light that shouldn't have been able to transmit through a digital signal. It spoke a single word.

The sound wasn't language. It was pressure—a weight that pushed through the speakers and settled behind Lilith's eyes like fingers pressing into her skull. The analysts dropped where they stood. Vasquez stumbled, caught herself on the doorframe. Somewhere down the corridor, someone began to scream.

Lilith felt it. She felt it, and she absorbed it. The pressure hit her like a wave—and vanished. Swallowed by the void inside her chest. The empty place where faith should have been. The reservoir that had been filling, unknown to her, since 2020. When her vision cleared, she was the only one still standing. The screen blazed with golden fire. Then it went dark.

Vasquez was on her knees, blood streaming from her nose. "What—" Her voice cracked. "What the fuck was that?"

Lilith reached down and pulled her up. Vasquez stared at her like she was seeing a stranger wearing a familiar face.

"You didn't fall," Vasquez whispered.

"No."

"Everyone falls. That was—that was Him. That was—"

"I know what it was."

Lilith started walking. After a moment, Vasquez followed, her steps unsteady.

The command center was built to survive anything. Underground. Reinforced. Hardened against nuclear strike and divine intervention alike. The kind of room where the last orders would be given when everything else had failed. It wouldn't survive this, Lilith thought. Nothing would.

The main screen displayed a global map. Red markers bloomed across every continent—each one a disabled installation, a grounded fleet, a silenced command post. The map was hemorrhaging crimson.

General Morrison stood at the center, phone pressed to his ear. His face might have been carved from granite. He wasn't speaking—just listening. Then he set the phone down. The room went quiet.

"Joint Chiefs just confirmed." His voice was hollow, scraped clean of emotion. "Naval Command. Strategic Air. CENTCOM. EUCOM. PACOM." He looked at the bleeding map. "They're not responding to us anymore."

Silence stretched like a held breath.

"What about—" someone started.

"No one's responding." Morrison's voice cut through the room. "No one. Every asset we had outside this facility is either dark or disabled. Everything we built. Gone."

Lilith stepped forward. Heads turned. She saw it in their faces—that same look. Not anger. Not blame. Fear.

"Not everything," she said.

Morrison's eyes found hers. The general who had signed off on her recruitment. Who had watched her test results with clinical interest rather than horror. Who had told her, eighteen months ago, that she was exactly the kind of weapon America needed.

"Blackwell." His voice was flat. "You killed an angel tonight."

"Yes, sir."

"And this—" He gestured at the bleeding map, the dead radios, the controlled panic filling the room. "This is because of you."

Lilith didn't look away. Didn't apologize. Didn't explain. "Yes, sir," she said again.

Morrison stared at her for a long moment. Then, impossibly, he laughed—a broken sound, the laugh of a man watching the world burn and finding something darkly funny in the flames. "Well," he said. "At least we know they can die."

In the quiet hours before dawn, Lilith sat alone in a corner of the command center and listened to the world come apart. Radio chatter. Emergency broadcasts. Fragments of a dying order bleeding through speakers no one had thought to mute. Confirmed, the Vatican is saying He appeared, physically manifested. China's still fighting, they have something, Aether-based weapons. Casualties in the millions, no response from international relief. Japan's holding, don't know how, some kind of containment field. Russia's gone dark but we're picking up weapons signatures, someone's still shooting.

She absorbed it all. Catalogued it. Filed it away in the cold, ordered spaces of her mind. The world was ending. Governments collapsing. Billions of people looking up at a sky that had just proven their prayers had a listener—and that listener was furious. Because of her. Because she had driven a knife through an angel's heart and shown the universe that gods could bleed.

A technician approached her station. Young. Pale. He set a cup of coffee beside her that she hadn't asked for, and his hands shook badly enough to slosh liquid over the rim.

"Ma'am." His voice was barely a whisper. "What... what happens now?"

Lilith looked at the map. Red everywhere. Cities dark. Nations silent. But not everywhere. Four points of light remained. Four nations still transmitting. Four nations with Aether technology and the will to use it. Four nations that hadn't learned how to kneel.

She picked up the coffee. Took a sip. It was bitter and lukewarm—exactly what she expected from a facility running on emergency power and terror.

"Now," she said, "we find out what God does when He's angry."

The technician fled.

Lilith smiled.

It wasn't a kind smile. It wasn't meant to be.
